# 2.1 Growing the Business

## Chapter Summary

This chapter explains how larger and growing businesses expand, raise finance, respond to changing objectives, and operate in national and international markets. Growth can happen internally through the business's own products, locations, technology and staff, or externally through mergers and takeovers. As businesses become larger, they may become public limited companies and gain access to wider sources of finance, but they also face more scrutiny, takeover risk and separation between owners and managers.

The chapter also shows that growth decisions are affected by globalisation, trade barriers, ethics, environmental responsibilities and pressure groups. Businesses must balance profit, competitiveness and stakeholder expectations.

## Key Concepts

- Business growth: an increase in size, measured by sales, profit, output, employees, locations, market share or business value.
- Internal or organic growth: expansion from within, such as new products, new locations, e-commerce, extra capacity or staff training.
- External or inorganic growth: expansion by merger or takeover.
- Takeover: one business buys control of another, usually by owning more than half of its shares.
- Merger: two businesses agree to join together to form one business.
- Public limited company (plc): a limited company whose shares can be bought by the public on a stock exchange.
- Retained profit: profit kept in the business for reinvestment instead of being paid out as dividends.
- Flotation: selling shares on the stock market to raise capital.
- Rights issue: offering new shares first to existing shareholders.
- Globalisation: increased integration of trade, finance, production and communication between countries.
- Tariff: a tax on imports.
- Trade bloc: a group of countries with free trade between members, often with barriers against non-members.
- Ethics: decisions based on what is morally right, not only what is most profitable.
- Sustainability: meeting present production needs without damaging future ability to produce.
- Pressure group: an organisation campaigning to change business or government behaviour.

## Key Arguments

- Growth can improve sales, profits, market share and economies of scale, but it can also increase complexity, risk and inefficiency.
- Organic growth is usually lower risk because it builds on existing strengths, but it may be slow and limited by market growth.
- External growth is faster and can reduce competition, but mergers and takeovers may suffer from culture clashes, high costs, redundancies and diseconomies of scale.
- Becoming a plc can raise large amounts of permanent finance, but it brings higher administration costs, public reporting and possible loss of control.
- The best source of finance depends on profitability, legal structure, risk, management record, purpose and time scale.
- Objectives change because of market conditions, technology, legislation, ownership, management and financial performance.
- Globalisation creates bigger markets and cheaper supply options, but also increases competition and exposes firms to world economic changes.
- Ethical and environmental behaviour can strengthen brand reputation and staff loyalty, but may increase costs and reduce short-term profit.
- Pressure groups can force changes to product, price, promotion and place by damaging reputation or influencing public opinion.

## Examples From the Chapter

- Just Eat grew organically by using e-commerce and smartphone ordering to link customers with takeaways.
- Aldi increased sales through its marketing mix, own-brand positioning and "Swap & Save" style campaigns.
- Premier Inn expanded organically by opening and refurbishing hotels in UK cities.
- Apple used research and development to improve products and launch new ones, supporting growth.
- Yorkshire Pianos exported to China after identifying demand for British-made pianos.
- Facebook bought WhatsApp to gain users, younger customers and international reach.
- Microsoft bought LinkedIn to strengthen its business software and network position.
- Heinz and Kraft merged to create a large food company.
- Freestyle Xtreme used online marketplaces and e-commerce to expand internationally.
- The Body Shop takeover by L'Oreal illustrates how ownership change may affect objectives.
- Fair Trade shows how firms may pay higher supplier prices for ethical reasons.
- Pressure group campaigns on drug prices, petrol prices, train fares, animal testing and local post office closures show how stakeholders can influence the marketing mix.

## Action Points / Practical Takeaways

- When judging growth, identify the measure used: sales, profit, employees, outlets, output, market share or value.
- For exam answers, compare organic and external growth using risk, speed, cost, control and market conditions.
- Match sources of finance to purpose: overdrafts for short-term cash needs, loans or share capital for long-term growth, retained profits when available.
- Explain why objectives change by linking the cause, effect on the current objective and the new likely objective.
- In international marketing, adapt the 4Ps to local tastes, income, culture, law, logistics and exchange/trade costs.
- Treat ethics and sustainability as trade-offs: higher costs may be justified by reputation, loyalty and long-term survival.
- Remember that pressure groups affect real business decisions because reputation can influence sales and brand value.
