# 2.5 Human Resource Decisions

## Chapter Summary

This chapter explains how growing businesses organise, recruit, train and motivate their workforce. As a business grows, it needs structures that clarify authority, responsibility, communication and decision-making. The chapter compares flat and hierarchical structures, centralised and decentralised decision-making, and different employment contracts.

It also explains the recruitment process, including job analysis, job descriptions, person specifications, internal and external recruitment, applications and selection. Training and development improve skills, productivity, quality and motivation. Finally, motivation is covered through financial and non-financial methods, showing that pay matters but is not the only way to improve employee performance.

## Key Concepts

- Organisational structure: how employees and roles are arranged in a business.
- Organisation chart: a diagram showing hierarchy, roles and reporting lines.
- Hierarchy: the levels of authority in a business.
- Span of control: the number of employees directly managed by one manager.
- Chain of command: the route through which authority and communication pass.
- Flat structure: few layers, short chains of command and wide spans of control.
- Hierarchical or tall structure: many layers, long chains of command and narrow spans of control.
- Delayering: removing a layer of management to reduce costs and shorten communication.
- Centralisation: decision-making kept mainly at the top or headquarters.
- Decentralisation: decision-making delegated to lower levels or local units.
- Communication barriers: factors that prevent or distort messages, such as language, noise, overload, emotion, gaps and inconsistency.
- Permanent, temporary, freelance, full-time, part-time and zero-hour contracts: different ways of organising work.
- Remote working: working away from business premises using technology.
- Recruitment: attracting and selecting workers.
- Job analysis: deciding whether a vacancy is needed and what type of worker is required.
- Job description: the roles and responsibilities of the post.
- Person specification: the skills, qualifications, experience and attributes needed.
- Internal recruitment: filling a vacancy from within the existing workforce.
- External recruitment: filling a vacancy from outside the business.
- Training: increasing employee knowledge and skills so they can perform effectively.
- Performance review or appraisal: a review of employee performance, targets and training needs.
- Motivation: the will to work.
- Financial motivation: money-based rewards such as wages, salary, commission and bonus.
- Non-financial motivation: methods such as job enlargement, job enrichment and autonomy.

## Key Arguments

- Organisational structure affects control, communication, cost and motivation.
- A wider span of control can reduce management costs and shorten communication, but may reduce supervision.
- A tall hierarchy can improve control, but communication may be slower and promotion layers more costly.
- Delayering can reduce costs and improve communication, but may reduce promotion opportunities and increase workload.
- Centralisation improves consistency, control and economies of scale, but can reduce local flexibility and manager motivation.
- Decentralisation improves local responsiveness, customer service and junior manager development, but can make control and consistency harder.
- Communication must be clear and sufficient; too little causes confusion and low motivation, while too much creates overload and inefficiency.
- Different contracts create trade-offs between business flexibility and worker security.
- Remote working can reduce premises and travel costs, but can make monitoring, motivation and work-life separation harder.
- Effective recruitment improves productivity, quality, customer service and staff retention.
- Internal recruitment is cheaper and motivating, but can limit new ideas; external recruitment widens the candidate pool but costs more and takes longer.
- Training is costly, but can improve quality, productivity, flexibility, motivation and ability to use new technology.
- Motivation improves productivity, attendance, staff retention, industrial relations, reputation, quality and customer service.
- Financial rewards are important, but non-financial methods can also improve commitment and performance.

## Examples From the Chapter

- A managing director with functional directors below them illustrates a typical organisational chart.
- Fast-food businesses often use centralisation to keep quality and customer experience consistent across many outlets.
- Supermarkets often use decentralisation because store managers need some local control, such as staffing decisions.
- Hotel chains decentralise some decisions so local managers can respond quickly to customer complaints.
- Open-plan offices can encourage openness but also create noise and distractions.
- Freelance musicians are used as an example of freelance contracts for concerts or tours.
- Zero-hour contracts are common in catering, farming and the health service because demand can vary.
- Remote working allows employees to combine work with domestic responsibilities but can also feel isolating.
- Recruitment agencies may specialise in areas such as nursing, financial services and teaching.
- Websites such as general job boards and employer websites are used for external recruitment.
- Airline pilots need significant off-the-job training before flying, showing why training method depends on role.
- Supplier-led training for new ICT systems shows how new technology can create retraining needs.
- Piece-rate pay is illustrated by fruit picking, while commission is linked to sales performance.

## Action Points / Practical Takeaways

- In structure questions, link flat/tall or centralised/decentralised choices to communication, control, cost and customer needs.
- Use span of control and chain of command accurately when interpreting organisation charts.
- For delayering, include both cost savings and possible motivation or workload problems.
- For communication, identify the barrier and explain its effect on motivation, speed, quality or decision-making.
- For contracts, compare flexibility for the business with security and motivation for the employee.
- For recruitment questions, follow the process: job analysis, job description, person specification, advertisement, applications, shortlisting, interview/tests, offer and contract.
- Compare internal and external recruitment using cost, speed, motivation, risk and fresh ideas.
- For training, match method to the job, budget, time and worker needs.
- In motivation answers, explain how the method affects productivity, retention, absenteeism or quality.
- Avoid assuming money is always enough; combine financial and non-financial methods where appropriate.
