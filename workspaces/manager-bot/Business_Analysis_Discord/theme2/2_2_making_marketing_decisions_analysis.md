# 2.2 Making Marketing Decisions

## Chapter Summary

This chapter explains how growing businesses use the marketing mix to build demand and competitive advantage. It focuses on product decisions, pricing, promotion and place. A successful business must design products that balance function, appearance and cost, manage the product life cycle, differentiate itself from competitors, choose suitable pricing strategies, promote effectively and make products available through the right distribution channels.

The key message is that the 4Ps must work together. A premium product needs a different price, promotion and distribution strategy from a basic product. Marketing decisions also need to change as competition, customer needs, technology and the product life cycle change.

## Key Concepts

- Marketing mix: the combination of product, price, promotion and place.
- Design mix: the balance between function, aesthetics and cost.
- Function: how well the product performs its purpose.
- Aesthetics: how the product looks and feels to customers.
- Cost: whether the product can be made and sold profitably.
- Product life cycle: introduction, growth, maturity and decline.
- Extension strategy: a method used to prolong product sales before decline.
- Product differentiation: making a product stand out from rivals.
- Unique selling point (USP): a feature that makes a product clearly different.
- Brand: a recognised product identity with a consistent image or character.
- Brand extension: using an existing brand for a related new product.
- Brand stretching: using a brand across a wider, less closely connected range.
- Promotional mix: the combination of methods used to communicate with customers.
- Advertising: paid-for promotion using media.
- Sales promotion: short-term point-of-sale tactics to boost sales.
- Distribution channel: the route a product takes from producer to consumer.
- Intermediary: a business between producer and consumer, such as a wholesaler or retailer.
- Direct selling: selling straight to customers without intermediaries.

## Key Arguments

- Good design is not just attractive; it must balance usefulness, appearance and profit potential.
- Products with strong function may have longer life cycles and stable demand, while aesthetic products may gain high added value but face imitation and shorter life cycles.
- Extension strategies can delay decline, but cannot save products that are obsolete or no longer wanted.
- Differentiation and strong branding can support higher prices and repeat purchases.
- Pricing decisions are difficult because a higher price may raise margin but reduce demand.
- Pricing depends on objectives, competitors, customers, costs, product life cycle, market conditions, the economy, bargaining power and legislation.
- Promotion must inform, persuade, remind and build brand image, but the best method depends on budget, product, customer, competition and life cycle stage.
- Technology makes promotion cheaper, more targeted and more interactive, especially through social media, apps and e-commerce.
- Distribution matters because customers must be able to buy the product in the right place, at the right time and in the right quantity.
- Competitive advantage comes from an integrated and flexible marketing mix, not from one isolated decision.

## Examples From the Chapter

- Technology products such as smartphones often launch at high prices because early adopters value new features.
- A hotel chain reduced prices during weaker demand and increased occupancy, revenue and profit.
- A fruit and vegetable supplier raised prices, lost supermarket orders and saw sales fall sharply.
- Dyson vacuum cleaners, Head and Shoulders shampoo and Rolls Royce cars are examples of products with strong USPs.
- Tesco Finest and Sainsbury Taste the Difference show retailer own-label branding.
- Microsoft, Coca-Cola, Disney, Mercedes and Hewlett Packard are examples of global brands.
- Dove soap and Dove shampoo show brand extension.
- Virgin trains and Virgin media show brand stretching.
- Netflix and Amazon Prime use product trials to encourage later paid purchases.
- Loyalty cards collect customer data as well as encouraging repeat purchases.
- Chocolate displays near tills show point-of-sale sales promotion.
- Holiday companies increasingly use direct online distribution instead of travel agents.
- Ford's heated front windscreen is used as an example of a protected competitive advantage.
- Go Compare changed its promotional campaign, then returned to its memorable singer after reduced effectiveness.

## Action Points / Practical Takeaways

- For product questions, link design mix choices to customer needs, costs and differentiation.
- For life cycle questions, identify the stage first, then recommend a suitable price, promotion or extension strategy.
- For pricing questions, always discuss both demand and profit margin.
- For promotion questions, match the method to the target market and product type.
- For distribution questions, compare control, cost, coverage, expertise and customer expectations.
- Use the 4Ps together: a luxury product needs premium pricing, selective distribution and image-based promotion; a basic product may compete more on price and availability.
- To explain competitive advantage, show how a marketing mix makes the business more attractive than rivals.
