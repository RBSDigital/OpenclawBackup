# 2.3 Making Operational Decisions

## Chapter Summary

This chapter explains how businesses manage production, suppliers, quality, sales and customer service. Operations turn inputs into goods and services, but the best method depends on scale, customer needs, cost, flexibility and quality requirements. Job, batch and flow production each suit different situations.

The chapter also shows how productivity, technology, stock control, procurement and logistics affect competitiveness. Good quality and customer service reduce costs, support repeat sales and strengthen brand reputation, while poor quality or weak service can quickly damage a business.

## Key Concepts

- Operations: the activities that transform inputs into goods and services.
- Job production: making one-off or small numbers of customised items.
- Batch production: making groups of similar items together.
- Flow production: continuous production of high volumes using a production line.
- Productivity: output produced per unit of input, such as per worker.
- Production technology: computerised or automated systems used in design or manufacturing.
- Supplier: a business that provides goods or services needed by another business.
- Stock control: managing stock so the business has enough to operate without holding excessive stock.
- Maximum stock level: the most stock a business wants or can hold.
- Re-order level: the point at which new stock should be ordered.
- Lead time: time between placing an order and receiving it.
- Buffer stock: minimum stock kept in case of unexpected demand or delays.
- Just in time (JIT): stock system where supplies arrive only when needed.
- Procurement: choosing and managing suppliers and orders.
- Logistics: planning and controlling the movement and storage of goods and information.
- Quality: meeting customer needs and expectations.
- Quality control: checking finished products or samples at the end of production.
- Quality assurance: building quality into every stage of production.
- Customer service: how a business looks after customers before, during and after purchase.

## Key Arguments

- Job production can give high quality and customisation, but unit costs are high and skilled labour is needed.
- Batch production reduces unit costs while allowing variation, but switching between batches can reduce efficiency.
- Flow production is efficient for mass markets, but set-up costs are high and work can become repetitive.
- Improving productivity is not the same as increasing total production; productivity is about efficiency and lower average cost.
- Technology can increase speed, consistency and flexibility, but it has high upfront costs and breakdown risk.
- Good supplier relationships are essential because poor quality or late deliveries affect output, reputation and customer service.
- JIT reduces stock holding costs and waste, but it depends heavily on reliable suppliers and predictable demand.
- Procurement decisions must balance cost, quality, delivery, availability, trust, customer expectations and cash flow.
- Quality assurance is usually more proactive than quality control because it aims for "right first time" throughout the process.
- Good sales processes and customer service increase loyalty, repeat purchases and reputation; poor service spreads quickly, especially online.

## Examples From the Chapter

- Job production examples include haircuts, wedding cakes, made-to-measure clothing and bridge building.
- Batch production examples include furniture, baking, meal preparation and clothing.
- Flow production is illustrated by car assembly lines and mass-market products such as Heinz baked beans.
- Car manufacturing uses computer-programmed machinery to add variations while still producing at scale.
- A stock control graph example uses 800 units maximum stock, 400 units reorder level, just under one week lead time and 200 units buffer stock.
- A car plant using JIT might receive the exact tyres needed for one day's production at a specific loading bay and time.
- Expensive restaurants need higher-quality suppliers because customer expectations are high.
- Digital delivery updates help manage customer expectations in logistics.
- Budget airlines and low-cost supermarkets show that "basic" products can still offer value for money.
- Amazon is used as an example of prioritising a fast selling process.
- Airport duty-free shops and tourist destinations often route visitors through shops to encourage unplanned sales.

## Action Points / Practical Takeaways

- Match production method to the market: customised products suit job production; varied but repeated products suit batch; high-volume standard products suit flow.
- In productivity answers, explain the link between output per worker, average costs, price competitiveness and profit margin.
- For technology decisions, weigh upfront cost and downtime against speed, flexibility, quality and lower labour costs.
- For stock questions, use the terms maximum stock, reorder level, lead time and buffer stock accurately.
- For JIT questions, mention both lower stock costs and the need for reliable nearby suppliers.
- For procurement questions, avoid choosing only the cheapest supplier; consider quality, reliability and customer expectations.
- For quality questions, compare quality control as inspection with quality assurance as prevention.
- For customer service questions, link satisfaction to repeat purchase, loyalty, word of mouth and lower complaint costs.
