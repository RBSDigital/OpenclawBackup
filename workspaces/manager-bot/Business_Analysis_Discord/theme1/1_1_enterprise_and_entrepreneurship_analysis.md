# 1.1 Enterprise and Entrepreneurship

## Chapter Summary

Business is dynamic: markets, technology, customer tastes, laws and economic conditions keep changing. Entrepreneurs spot opportunities from these changes, from original ideas, from adapting existing products, from industry experience, personal experience, hobbies, complaints and observation.

Enterprise involves taking a calculated risk to set up and run a business. The possible rewards include profit, independence, personal satisfaction and business growth, but the risks include failure, loss of investment, cash-flow problems and personal liability for sole traders and partnerships.

## Key Concepts

- **Dynamic market**: a market that changes over time because of technology, consumer wants, laws, competition, fashion or economic conditions.
- **Obsolete product**: a product that becomes outdated because customer needs or technology have moved on.
- **Entrepreneur**: someone who takes a calculated risk by starting and running a business.
- **Enterprise**: business activity that involves organising resources, taking risks and creating goods or services.
- **Risk**: the chance that something goes wrong, causing loss or damage.
- **Reward**: the benefit from enterprise, such as profit, independence or satisfaction.
- **Added value**: the difference between the selling price and the cost of producing a product.
- **USP**: a unique selling point that makes a product different from competitors.

## Key Arguments

- Change creates opportunities for alert entrepreneurs but threats for businesses that stand still.
- A good business idea normally solves a problem, meets customer needs and can be delivered profitably.
- Business experience can reduce start-up risk, but too much familiarity may limit fresh thinking.
- Risk cannot be removed, but planning, research, careful finance and regular review can reduce it.
- Entrepreneurs need a mix of qualities such as innovation, determination, organisation, persuasion and leadership, but they rarely possess every strength.

## Examples From the Chapter

- **Propercorn**: used changing consumer tastes for healthier snacks to create a healthier popcorn brand.
- **King of Shaves**: developed from personal frustration with painful wet shaving.
- **Mallzee**: responded to the inconvenience of browsing many clothing websites separately.
- **Superjam**: grew from a family recipe for sugar-free jam.
- **Green Thumb**: copied and adapted a lawn-care service idea observed in the USA for the UK market.
- **Sarah Willingham**: bought and developed The Bombay Bicycle Club into a successful restaurant chain.

## Action Points / Practical Takeaways

- Look for business ideas in customer complaints, queues, poor service and changing trends.
- Test ideas with market research before investing heavily.
- Avoid over-optimistic assumptions about sales, costs and demand.
- Keep costs and borrowing under control in the start-up stage.
- Monitor progress against aims and review risks regularly.
- Build on strengths, but recruit or seek support where the entrepreneur lacks skills.
