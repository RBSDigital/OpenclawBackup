# 1.4 Making the Business Effective

## Chapter Summary

Making a business effective means choosing a suitable legal structure, location, marketing mix and business plan. Entrepreneurs must understand the difference between unlimited liability and limited liability, then choose between options such as sole trader, partnership, private limited company or franchise.

Location affects costs, access to customers, labour, raw materials, communication links and competition. The marketing mix combines product, price, place and promotion and must fit the target market. A business plan brings these decisions together and helps the entrepreneur test whether the idea is realistic and financially viable.

## Key Concepts

- **Unlimited liability**: the owner is personally responsible for business debts.
- **Limited liability**: shareholders can only lose the money they invested in shares.
- **Sole trader**: a business owned and controlled by one person.
- **Partnership**: a business owned by two or more people.
- **Private limited company (Ltd)**: a company with separate legal identity and shareholders.
- **Franchise**: a licence allowing a franchisee to trade using a franchisor's brand and business format.
- **Marketing mix**: product, price, place and promotion.
- **Business plan**: a written document setting out the business idea, objectives, strategy, market and financial forecasts.

## Key Arguments

- Legal structure affects risk, control, finance, paperwork and continuity.
- Sole traders are simple and quick to set up, but carry unlimited liability and can struggle to raise finance.
- Private limited companies protect shareholders through limited liability, but bring more administration and public disclosure.
- Franchising can reduce risk because the format is tested, but it limits freedom and can be expensive.
- Location decisions depend on cost, customers, labour, raw materials, government help, communications and competition.
- The marketing mix must be balanced: changing one element affects the others.
- A business plan reduces risk, helps raise finance and provides a benchmark for reviewing performance.

## Examples From the Chapter

- **Service sole traders**: hairdressers, gardeners, plumbers and electricians often use the sole trader structure.
- **Professional partnerships**: doctors, solicitors and vets are common partnership examples.
- **Private limited companies**: examples include Iceland, Virgin, John Lewis and Clarks.
- **Franchise brands**: Subway, McDonald's, Starbucks, Pizza Hut, Thorntons, KFC and Europcar.
- **Coffee shop location**: needs a strong customer-facing site rather than a home base.
- **E-commerce in electrical goods**: made price and delivery more important than physical retail outlets.
- **Fitbit**: shows how product, high price and promotion can work together in the keep-fit market.

## Action Points / Practical Takeaways

- Choose the legal structure that matches the risk, finance needs and desired level of control.
- Use a partnership agreement if forming a partnership.
- Research any franchise before investing, even if the brand seems established.
- Compare location options using costs, customer access, labour, competition and future expansion needs.
- Keep the marketing mix consistent with the target market.
- Update the marketing mix when technology, competition or customer needs change.
- Write a business plan that includes objectives, market research, finance needs, operations, marketing mix, cash-flow and profit forecasts.
